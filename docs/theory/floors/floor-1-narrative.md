# Floor 1 — Narrative

Floor notes series, 12 August 2026. Companions: §3.1 (definition), §3.4 (who works it), §3.5
(signs), the risers image (one desk at the top), and the floor-presentations bank.

---

## The essence: the binding story

The paper's definition: "the binding story, the single 'you' stretched across time." Floor 1 is
not a collection of beliefs — it is the *arc* that arranges everything below it into one
biography. The risers image says it structurally: **one desk at the top**, because however many
dials a person wears, one story has to bind all ten risers into a single life. The narrative is
a prediction too — about what kind of person this is, what happens to people like him, how this
sort of life goes — and like every prediction, it filters what gets filed. The story is not a
record of the evidence; it is the clerk that decides what counts as evidence.

## Against its neighbors

- **Versus floor 3 (beliefs):** a belief is a proposition — *"I'm lazy"* — arguable sentence by
  sentence. The narrative is the plot that survives losing any single proposition. You can
  correct a belief and watch the story reabsorb the correction as a subplot ("okay, I finished
  that one thing — the exception that proves it"). That reabsorption is the floor-1 tell: the
  argument was won on 3 and metabolized by 1.
- **Versus floor 2 (metacognition):** floor 2 holds beliefs about the *machinery* (are thoughts
  facts, are feelings dangerous); floor 1 holds the *plot*. "People don't change" is floor 2
  when it's a claim about minds; "people like me don't get second acts" is floor 1 — the same
  pessimism, wearing a story.
- **Versus floor 8:** the master narratives the story is written *in* — what counts as a good
  life, a real man, a dutiful daughter — are the water, not the desk. Floor 1 writes with
  floor 8's vocabulary.

## How it gets written

By telling. The family assigns roles early ("the smart one," "the screwup"), and a childhood of
retellings settles them; later the person retells themselves — every anecdote chosen at a
party, every self-summary at an intake, another pass of the pen. Written and rewritten in
language, which is why it is the most glass of all the floors: genuinely reachable by
conversation, and genuinely load-bearing anyway.

## What reaches it — and why it works

§3.4 assigns **narrative therapy and logotherapy**; SFBT works the same seam. The mechanism, in
engine terms, is a fight against one specific furnace move: **the filter at story scale.** A
problem-saturated story has discarded every exception; re-authoring systematically hunts the
discarded disconfirming evidence — the exceptions, the unique outcomes — and re-weights it.
Externalizing ("the problem is the problem; the person is not the problem") is defusion
performed at the level of identity. Logotherapy and existential work handle the other floor-1
job: when the story must be built around facts that will not move (the givens — see the
existential modality note), re-authoring the *meaning-frame* rather than the facts.

Failure modes, informatively: re-authoring over an unregulated concrete floor produces a nicer
story and an unchanged alarm — the floors below must be workable before the new story can be
lived rather than recited. And a future-story with no path built to it (the miracle question,
unfinished) stays inspirational.

## From your chair

Signs: successes narrated as flukes while failures slot home instantly; the story that cannot be
told (gaps, scrambled timeline); identity questions after a rupture. And the furnace connection
— **the story that starts at event two** (§3.5's listening sign) is floor 1 running defense for
the furnace: an account accurate from its second event onward, wrong only about the first cause.

The five presentations (composites):

1. "I'm the screwup of the family. Always have been." Every new failure slots into a story that
   predates it; successes are flukes.
2. The story that cannot be told: gaps, scrambled timeline, "I don't remember much of my
   childhood" — the incoherence itself is the presentation.
3. "Since the divorce I don't know who I am anymore." The story stopped; nothing binds the days.
4. The eulogy problem: asked what the life should have been about, nothing comes — chapters
   exist, the book does not.
5. "The diagnosis explains everything about me." A borrowed story swallowing the person.

## Ten in the wild

All composites. The floor-1 tell in each: the *plot* is doing the work — sorting events into a
story that predates them.

1. A man gets promoted and tells his wife "they'll figure out soon that they made a mistake."
   Not anxiety — plot consistency. Success has no slot in the story, so it files as error.
2. A woman recounting her week files every good thing as luck and every bad thing as "classic
   me." Same events, two different clerks.
3. A man whose brother died at nineteen has organized thirty years around "living for both of
   us." Retirement terrifies him — the assignment has no ending written.
4. "I was a wife. I don't know what I am now." The days feel unstrung since the divorce — the
   story stopped mid-book, and nothing is binding the chapters.
5. A man still introduces himself through his failure — "I'm the guy who tanked the startup" —
   five years later. The worst chapter got promoted to the title.
6. A woman diagnosed with ADHD at forty re-narrates her whole life through the label: relief
   for a month, then the label starts swallowing — every trait re-filed as symptom.
7. A man can't tell his childhood in order — islands of scenes, no bridges. He shrugs: "not
   much to tell." The gaps are the presentation.
8. An immigrant's daughter carries "we sacrificed everything so you could—" as the opening
   clause of every decision; her own preferences read to her as plot betrayal.
9. At his father's funeral a man catches himself rehearsing what will be said about *his* life
   — and finds chapters but no through-line. The eulogy problem, arriving live.
10. A woman sober for eight years keeps dreaming of relapse — "addict" organized a decade of
    meetings and mornings, and the story hasn't been given a next chapter. The old plot pulls
    because it is at least coherent.

## In the machinery

Floor 1 strains visibly when the dials disagree — competent at work, catastrophic at home — and
the desk must write one book from ten inconsistent risers: *"I don't know which one is really
me"* is not a philosophical problem but a floor-1 workload problem. Narrative coherence work is
shared-floor work: it transfers across every riser, because every riser's material passes
through the same desk.
