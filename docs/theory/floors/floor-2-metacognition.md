# Floor 2 — Metacognition

Floor notes series, 12 August 2026. Companions: §3.1, §3.4, §3.5, the floor-presentations bank.

---

## The essence: beliefs about your own mind

The paper's definition: "beliefs about your own mind — whether thoughts are facts, whether
feelings are dangerous, whether change is possible." Floor 2 holds no beliefs about the world;
it holds the **operating policies for the machinery itself**. Are thoughts reports or events?
Are feelings weather or floods? Is attention a searchlight I aim, or a place things happen to
me? These are predictions like everything else — but their subject is the building, which gives
them outsized leverage: a floor-2 setting decides how everything on every other floor gets
handled.

## Against its neighbors

- **Versus floor 3:** "I'm worthless" is floor 3 — a belief about the self. "If I let myself
  feel this, it will never stop" is floor 2 — a belief about *feelings as a class*. Same
  sentence-shaped format, different subject matter, and the difference picks the tool: floor 3
  wants the content examined; floor 2 wants the *relationship to content* changed.
- **Versus floor 1:** "people don't change" is floor 2 when it's a claim about minds in
  general; it's floor 1 when it's a chapter of this person's plot.
- **Versus the valve (§2.2):** this is the deep connection. Floor 2 is where the valve's
  *policies* are written. "Worrying is how I stay ready" is a precision policy — deliberate
  high gain on threat — held as a belief. Thought-action fusion is a precision policy: mental
  events granted the weight of acts. The prepayment pattern (§2.3's new paragraph) is usually
  administered from here.

## How it gets written

By how the household handled minds. Whether feelings were named or punished; whether crying
stopped the room or emptied it; whether "I'm scared" was met with information or contempt;
whether anyone ever changed their mind out loud. A child whose perceptions were routinely
overwritten ("that didn't happen; you're too sensitive") writes the floor-2 entry *my read
cannot be trusted* — and arrives decades later asking the clinician to referee reality.

## What reaches it — and why it works

§3.4 assigns **metacognitive therapy and the mindfulness-based approaches**; ACT's defusion is
the same lever. The shared mechanism: *change the relationship to the thought, not its content.*
Watching a thought arise and pass — without obeying, arguing, or suppressing — is a direct
experiential disconfirmation of floor 2's central errors: thoughts turn out not to be facts,
feelings turn out to crest and fall rather than swallow. Note that this floor is reached by
**demonstration, not debate**: sixty seconds of watching a feeling peak and subside outweighs an
hour of arguing that feelings are safe, because the belief is about experience and only
experience can testify.

**Floor 2 is the gatekeeper floor.** Its settings decide whether the rest of therapy is even
possible: a client who believes feelings are bottomless cannot consent to floor-6 work; one who
believes change is impossible cannot weight any corrective evidence; one who believes worry is a
job will not surrender it until the job is re-described. When treatment stalls everywhere at
once, check floor 2 first — the problem may not be the content anywhere, but the policy over all
of it.

## From your chair

The five presentations (composites):

1. "If I let myself start crying, I'm afraid I'll never stop." Feelings as bottomless.
2. "What kind of person even thinks that?" A thought treated as a moral event — thought-action
   fusion presenting as shame about mental content.
3. "Worrying is how I stay ready. If I relax, that's when it happens." Worry held as a job.
4. "This is just who I am. People don't really change." Fixed-mind belief presenting as calm
   treatment skepticism, first session.
5. "I can't trust my own read on anything anymore." Brings witnesses; asks you to referee.

## Ten in the wild

All composites. The floor-2 tell in each: the belief is about the *machinery* — thoughts,
feelings, attention, change — not about the world.

1. A man won't start grieving his wife: "If I open that door, I'll never come back." Years of
   postponed grief, held shut by a belief about grief.
2. A new mother confesses "horrible thoughts" about dropping the baby — and is terrified the
   thought means she secretly wants to. Thought read as intent.
3. A man treats his 3am anxieties as briefings: "that's when the truth comes out." The anxious
   mind believed to be the honest one.
4. A woman keeps a worry vigil for every flight her son takes, quietly certain the one flight
   she doesn't worry about is the one that goes down.
5. A man refuses to feel hopeful before test results — hope "jinxes it." Good feelings believed
   to cause bad outcomes.
6. "I'm just not someone who can meditate — my brain doesn't turn off." Attention held as fixed
   hardware; every tool offered bounces off the belief about tools.
7. A woman apologizes for crying in session — "sorry, I'm being weak right now." Feeling states
   graded as moral performances.
8. A man reports one intrusive image and asks if he's going insane — a mind that produces ugly
   content believed to be a mind malfunctioning.
9. A woman told all childhood that she was imagining things now runs every perception past two
   friends before believing it. The belief "my read can't be trusted," installed by others.
10. "If I understand why I do it and still do it, I must not really want to change." A false
    theory of how change works, converting the ordinary architecture into a character verdict.

## In the machinery

Floor 2 is the panel's control-room: the valve of §2.2 takes its standing orders from here, and
the surprise readout (§6.2's one-line test) is gated by it — a client whose floor 2 forbids
surprise ("if I didn't predict it, I failed") will convert every violation into confirmation
before it can write. In the risers image, floor 2's green cells sit only under the dials whose
content includes beliefs about one's own states — exposure, need, desire — which is itself a
teaching point: not every domain routes through self-beliefs, but the ones that do are the ones
where the client polices their own interior.
